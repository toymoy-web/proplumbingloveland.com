Why This Gets Missed

Homeowners Usually Blame The Wrong Thing First

Low water pressure at every fixture in the house gets blamed on the fixtures themselves more often than it should. New faucet aerators, a new showerhead, even a new water heater sometimes get tried first, when the actual bottleneck is the underground line delivering water to the house in the first place. If pressure is down everywhere at once rather than at one specific fixture, that's the clue the problem sits upstream of the house's interior plumbing entirely, on the water line itself.

Older galvanized service lines are the ones most likely to end up here, for the same reason galvanized interior pipe does: the pipe narrows gradually from internal corrosion rather than failing all at once. It narrows gradually as rust builds up on the inside of the pipe wall. That's why falling pressure often shows up slowly over a year or two rather than as a single sudden event, and why it's easy to blame something else first. That gradual pattern is part of why homeowners often try replacing fixtures or a pressure regulator first, since a slow decline over months doesn't feel like the kind of problem a buried pipe would cause, even though that's exactly what it usually is.

Repair Vs. Replace

Trenchless Options Where The Site Allows

A single confirmed break in an otherwise sound line can sometimes be spot-repaired. A line showing corrosion or reduced flow along its full length, common in older galvanized service lines, usually calls for full replacement instead. Trenchless replacement pulls a new line through the old path using small access pits at each end, which works in most yards, though a driveway crossing, mature trees, or other buried utilities occasionally rule it out in favor of a traditional trench. The replacement pipe material and burial depth still follow the same Uniform Plumbing Code (UPC) requirements the city inspects against.

Service Lines Near The River

City Meter To House Along The Big Thompson

Loveland's water comes through the City of Loveland Water & Power system, and the underground line between the meter and your house is where many low-pressure problems begin. In older neighborhoods near downtown that line may be original, and homes near the Big Thompson corridor or irrigation ditch crossings can see ground movement over the decades. A pressure test at the meter tells us quickly whether the problem is the line or the plumbing inside.

Meter To House

Service Line Questions Callers Ask Most

Many callers describe the same picture. The meter keeps turning with every fixture off, a patch of lawn stays soggy, or a utility notice says water is escaping on the property side. When those signs line up, the line between the meter and the house is the first suspect.

Repair Or Replace?

One failed joint is a spot repair. A line that has leaked in several places, or one made of old galvanized or lead material, usually points to replacement. The age of the home and any utility records suggest what is buried. If lead is suspected, ask for the pipe material to be identified before you accept a quote.

Weak Pressure, Or No Water At All

No water with the bill paid has three likely causes. The main shutoff may be partly closed, the utility side may be interrupted, or the service line may have failed. Check the meter box and a neighbor first. If the meter is dry and the street is fine, call. A private well pump or pressure tank is a different system, and well work is not listed here, so mention it early on the phone.

Replacing The Main From Curb To House

Some callers need the whole run replaced after a break. That is the same work described above, scheduled with a locate, a permit, and utility coordination where required. A leak that started near the meter box may also relate to the leak detection steps for confirming the source.
