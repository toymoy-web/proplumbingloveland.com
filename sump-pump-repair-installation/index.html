What Usually Prompts This Call

Signs Your Sump Pump Is Failing

Pump Won't Start

Water is rising in the pit and nothing happens when the float lifts.

Runs Nonstop

Constant cycling often points to a stuck float, a failed check valve, or a pit that cannot keep up.

Grinding Or Rattling

Noise from the motor or impeller usually means wear, debris, or a failing bearing.

Musty Basement Or Damp Walls

Moisture, rust stains, or a damp smell can mean the pump is no longer holding groundwater down.

Why Pumps Fail

Most Failures Are Small Parts, Not The Whole Pump

A sump pump has one job, which is to move groundwater out of the pit before it reaches the floor. The parts that fail most often are the float switch, the check valve, and the discharge line, not the motor itself. A float that sticks against the pit wall keeps a healthy pump from ever turning on. A failed check valve lets water fall back into the pit after every cycle, so the pump runs again and again until it wears out early.

Winter adds its own failure mode. A discharge line that freezes or fills with ice has nowhere to send water, and the pump overheats trying. Heavy rain and spring runoff are the other peak times, and a pump that is already weak tends to quit exactly then. That is why so many sump calls come in the middle of a storm.

Before You Call: If Your Basement Is Taking On Water

  • Stay out of standing water if any outlet, extension cord, or appliance is in or near it. Water and live power are the real danger here.
  • If you can reach the breaker panel without stepping in water, switch off power to the basement circuits before you do anything else.
  • Do not pull or handle the pump while it is plugged in and sitting in water.
  • Move boxes, rugs, and stored items off the floor if it is safe to do so, then call our 24/7 emergency line.

Loveland sits where the Big Thompson River leaves the foothills, and water is part of the landscape here. Irrigation ditches, Lake Loveland, and Boyd Lake can also keep groundwater closer to the surface in some pockets during wet seasons and through the irrigation season.

We see sump calls in older basements around West Central Loveland and the blocks north of Lake Loveland, in homes near the Boyd Lake neighborhoods, and on sloped lots in Mariana Butte where runoff follows the hillside. Many newer homes in Centerra and The Lakes at Centerra also have a builder-installed pump, and it ages like any other.

Repair Or Replace

How We Decide

We test the float, the check valve, the discharge line, and the pump's electrical draw before recommending anything. A stuck float or a bad check valve is a repair. A pump past its typical 7 to 10 year life, with a seized impeller or a motor that runs hot, is usually cheaper to replace than to keep fixing. We tell you which one applies and quote a flat price before work begins.

Installations follow the International Plumbing Code (IPC) and the pump manufacturer's instructions, and electrical connections follow the National Electrical Code (NEC) and local amendments. We also recommend a dedicated circuit for the pump so a tripped breaker elsewhere cannot take it offline.

Keeping A Working Pump Working

A Two-Minute Check Before Storm Season

Once or twice a year, pour a bucket of water into the pit or lift the float by hand and confirm the pump starts, empties the pit, and shuts off. Look at the outdoor end of the discharge line in winter to make sure it is not buried in snow or capped with ice. If you have a battery backup, check its charge indicator and replace the battery on the manufacturer's schedule.

Spend a minute looking at the pit itself. Gravel, silt, and debris that wash in over time can jam the float or clog the intake, and a pump that sits in a dirty pit works harder than one in a clean pit. A quick cleanout before the wet season costs far less than a flooded basement.
