What Usually Prompts This Call

Signs Of A Hidden Leak

Unexplained High Water Bill

A jump in usage with no obvious new source at home.

Sound Of Running Water

An audible trickle or hiss with every fixture off.

Damp Or Discolored Spot

A wall, ceiling, or floor area that stays damp for no clear reason.

Musty Smell

A persistent odor without visible water is often moisture trapped behind a surface.

The Real Problem Behind The Symptom

A High Bill Isn't The Actual Issue

Most calls we get about this start with a symptom that isn't the leak itself, a water bill that doesn't match usage, a utility room that smells musty, or a spot on the ceiling that won't fully dry out. None of those tell you where the water is actually coming from, which is why guessing at a fix, like re-caulking a shower that isn't the real source, wastes time while the actual leak keeps running. A proper leak detection call starts with confirming there's an active leak at all, using a meter test, before spending any time trying to pinpoint its location.

Before You Call: What To Check

  • Turn off every fixture and appliance that uses water, then watch your water meter for a few minutes.
  • Do not attempt to cut into a wall or ceiling to chase a suspected leak yourself. It's easy to hit a pipe or wiring.
  • Note when you first noticed the bill jump, smell, or damp spot. It helps narrow down how long the leak has likely been active.
  • If you see active water pooling near an electrical outlet or panel, avoid the area and mention it when you call.

How We Find It

Acoustic And Thermal Detection

Once a leak is confirmed, acoustic listening equipment picks up the specific sound water makes escaping a pressurized line, even through a wall or under a floor, and can narrow the location without opening anything up. Thermal imaging complements this by spotting the temperature difference a leak creates behind a surface, since escaping water changes the surface temperature enough to show up on a thermal camera. Between the two methods, most leaks outside of a concrete slab are pinpointed without exploratory demolition.

Irrigation lines are worth a separate mention, since Loveland's mix of established lawns and newer landscaping means a fair number of the "unexplained high bill" calls we get actually trace back to a cracked sprinkler line rather than anything inside the house. That distinction matters for the fix, not just the diagnosis. An indoor leak usually means opening a wall or floor, while an irrigation leak is dug up and repaired in the yard, at a very different cost. Confirming which system the leak is actually on is one of the first things a meter test and a look at the irrigation valve helps settle.

Irrigated Lots And Older Plumbing

Where Hidden Leaks Hide Around Loveland

Larger irrigated lots in Mariana Butte and around Boyd Lake mean long runs of buried irrigation line, a common place for a slow leak that inflates the water bill without any visible wet spot. Inside older homes near downtown, the more likely culprit is a corroded supply line or a failing shutoff valve behind a wall. Starting with a meter test tells us which side of the house to search.

Questions Callers Ask First

Price, Equipment, And What Happens After You Call

Most people call with the same four questions. What will it cost, what equipment is used, how close can the search get, and can the same company fix it? Here are plain answers to each.

What Moves The Price

The size of the search drives the price. A single bathroom wall is a smaller job than a two-story home with a buried line. Slab, soil, and finished drywall each change how long the search takes. The dispatcher asks a few questions on the phone and gives a clear quote before work begins.

Equipment And Pinpointing

The detection steps above start with a meter test, then acoustic listening and thermal imaging. Our technicians carry current detection equipment, and pressure testing is added when a buried line is suspect and the meter alone cannot say which section is failing. Ask the technician to mark the spot on the surface and photograph it, so you have a record for an insurer, a landlord, or the water utility.

Finding It Is Only Half The Job

Once the source is confirmed, you get a flat price for the repair. A failed fitting or fixture connection may be handled in short order. A buried run is scheduled with its own quote, and the water line page explains that route. You can also keep the findings and hire someone else for the fix.

Inside The House

Ceilings, Walls, And Crawlspaces Hide Different Leaks

Stain On A Ceiling

The wet patch often sits several feet from the leak because water runs along joists before it drips.

Cool, Damp Wall

A wall that feels cold with no fixture nearby can hide a pinhole in a supply line.

Crawlspace Sounds

Water running under the floor with nothing on points to a failed joint or a cracked drain below the house.

Wet Drywall Near A Tub

A leak behind a shower wall may show up as soft drywall in the next room.

A dry-looking crawlspace can still hide a slow drip behind insulation, and a failing supply joint looks a lot like a cracked drain from below. Describing the sound, the stain, and the room helps decide where to start. If the crawlspace pipes are old and failing in more than one place, the repiping page covers the longer-term fix.
