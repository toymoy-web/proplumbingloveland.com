Common Failures

Signs Your Water Heater Needs Repair

No Hot Water At All

Often a failed heating element, thermocouple, or gas valve.

Lukewarm Water Only

One of two elements failed on an electric unit, or a partially closed gas valve.

Leaking Around The Base

Can mean a failed drain valve, a loose fitting, or a cracked tank.

Rumbling Or Popping

Sediment buildup on the tank bottom, worse with Loveland's hard water.

Emergency Response

If You Have No Hot Water Right Now

A dead water heater is one of our most common emergency calls, and it's dispatched with the same urgency as a burst pipe. Our emergency line is answered 24/7, including nights, weekends, and holidays, and a technician arrives with the parts to fix most common failures on the first visit rather than needing to order anything. If the tank is actively leaking, shutting off the cold water supply valve at the top of the unit before we arrive limits any water damage in the meantime.

Gas and electric units fail differently, which is why we ask a few questions on the phone before dispatching. A gas unit with no hot water is often a thermocouple that's stopped sensing pilot flame correctly, causing the gas valve to shut off as a safety measure. An electric unit is more often one of its two heating elements burning out, which shows up as lukewarm rather than fully cold water if only the lower element has failed. Neither diagnosis needs to happen over the phone. It just helps the technician bring the right parts on the first trip.

Any repair that touches the gas line, the temperature and pressure relief valve, or the venting follows the same International Plumbing Code (IPC) framework Colorado has adopted for new installs. A repair that skips those requirements to save time is the kind of shortcut that shows up as a real safety issue later, not just a code technicality.

Repair Vs. Replace

What Determines The Right Fix

A single failed part on a tank under 8-10 years old is almost always worth repairing rather than replacing. Thermocouples, heating elements, thermostats, and high-limit switches are common, inexpensive fixes on both gas and electric units. Once a tank is past 10-12 years old, has failed more than once in the past year, or is actively leaking from the tank body itself rather than a fitting, replacement is usually the better value, and we'll tell you plainly which situation you're in.
