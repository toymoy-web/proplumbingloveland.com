Why Does My Water Bill Keep Rising? 5 Loveland Causes

A water bill that keeps climbing with no obvious explanation is frustrating, but it almost always has a real, specific cause. Here are the ones we actually find.

By the Loveland Pro Plumbing team

An unexplained jump in your water bill isn't random. Water doesn't disappear, it goes somewhere, and in the large majority of cases that somewhere is a leak or a running fixture you haven't noticed yet. Here are the five causes we actually find most often when a Loveland homeowner calls about this.

1. A Running Toilet

This is the single most common cause of a mysteriously high water bill, and it's often silent. A worn flapper or fill valve that isn't sealing completely can let water run continuously into the bowl without an audible sound loud enough to notice day to day. A simple test: put a few drops of food coloring in the tank, wait 15 minutes without flushing, and check if color shows up in the bowl. If it does, water is leaking past the flapper. This is usually a quick, inexpensive toilet repair.

2. A Hidden Leak Behind A Wall Or Under A Floor

Unlike a running toilet, a hidden leak inside a wall or under a floor gives no visible sign at all until it's caused enough damage to show through drywall or flooring. The clearest way to check for this is a simple meter test: turn off every fixture and appliance that uses water, then check whether your water meter is still moving. If it is, water is going somewhere it shouldn't, and professional leak detection equipment can pinpoint it without opening up walls on a guess.

3. A Leaking Or Failing Water Service Line

This is a step further upstream than a leak inside the house. The underground line running from the street to your home can develop a leak of its own, especially in older galvanized service lines. A tell-tale combination is a rising bill alongside a wet or unusually green patch of yard somewhere between the street and the house. Water line repair or replacement is a different service from indoor leak detection, since the diagnostic approach for an underground line differs from one inside the home.

4. Irrigation System Leaks

If you have a sprinkler or irrigation system, it's worth ruling in or out separately from your home's indoor plumbing, since a cracked line underground can run for a long time before anyone notices a wet or soggy patch in the lawn. This is common enough in newer subdivisions across the area, including towns like Timnath where extensive irrigation systems are standard on newer builds, that it's always worth checking alongside indoor causes rather than assuming the leak has to be inside the house.

5. A Slab Leak

If you've ruled out running fixtures, checked the meter with everything off and it's still moving, and don't see any obvious wet spot in the yard, a leak under a concrete slab foundation is the next thing to consider, especially if you've noticed a warm spot on an otherwise room-temperature floor. Slab leak detection uses different equipment than standard leak detection, since concrete transmits sound and heat differently than drywall or framing does.

How To Narrow It Down Before You Call

Start with the free checks: the food coloring test for toilets, and the meter test with everything off. Those two alone rule in or out the two most common causes. If both come back clean but your bill is still climbing, that's when professional leak detection becomes the efficient next step, rather than guessing at which of the remaining causes applies.

A Sixth Possibility: Water Softener Regeneration

If you have a water softener, it's worth ruling this one in or out too. A softener that's regenerating more frequently than it should, often because it's undersized for actual household usage or hardness level, uses more water per cycle than a correctly sized unit would. This shows up as a real but often overlooked contributor to a rising bill, especially in a household that added a softener recently and hasn't compared usage before and after.

How Much Water Actually Is A Leak, In Real Terms

It helps to put this in concrete terms. A toilet flapper leaking slowly enough to be nearly silent can still waste over a hundred gallons a day, which adds up to thousands of gallons a month on a bill that otherwise looks unremarkable at a glance. A pinhole leak in a pressurized line can release even more, continuously, whether or not anyone is home to notice. These aren't dramatic, obvious leaks. They're the quiet kind that only show up as a number on a bill.

When To Call A Professional Versus Keep Checking Yourself

The two free checks, food coloring in the toilet tank and a meter test with everything off, take under half an hour combined and rule out the two most common causes. If both come back clean and the bill is still climbing, further self-diagnosis usually means guessing, since the remaining causes, irrigation leaks, service line issues, and slab leaks, all require equipment or expertise most homeowners don't have on hand. That's the point where a professional visit is the efficient next step rather than continuing to guess.

What To Have Ready When You Call

Having a few pieces of information ready speeds up the call and helps us prioritize how urgently to dispatch. Your last two or three water bills for comparison, whether you've noticed any wet spots indoors or in the yard, and the results of the meter test if you've already run it all help narrow down which of the causes above is most likely before a technician even arrives.

A Note On Seasonal Bill Changes

Not every increase points to a problem. Outdoor irrigation use during warmer months genuinely raises usage for most households, and a bill that climbs seasonally in a pattern that repeats year over year is often just normal outdoor watering rather than anything wrong. What's worth investigating is an increase that doesn't match your own usage pattern, whether that's a jump with no seasonal explanation or a bill that keeps climbing month over month rather than leveling off.

How Your Water Utility Can Help Too

Many water utilities, including the one serving Loveland, can pull your account's usage history and flag whether a specific billing period looks unusual compared to your own past pattern, not just a generic average household. That comparison is often more useful than trying to judge a single bill in isolation, since it accounts for your household's actual normal usage rather than a citywide average that may not reflect how your specific home uses water.

Don't Let It Go Too Long Either Way

Whether the cause turns out to be a simple flapper or something that needs professional detection equipment, the cost of waiting almost always outweighs the cost of checking. A leak that's wasting a hundred gallons a day today will still be wasting that much a month from now if it's left alone, and in the case of a hidden or slab leak, the water damage risk climbs the longer it runs undetected.
