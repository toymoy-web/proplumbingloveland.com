What To Check Before Your Home's Backflow Test

A little preparation before your backflow test appointment can be the difference between a quick pass and a failed test that needs a follow-up visit.

By the Loveland Pro Plumbing team

If you've received a notice that your backflow prevention assembly is due for its annual test, a few minutes of preparation beforehand can prevent a failed result that requires a return visit. None of this is complicated, but it's easy to overlook if you've never thought about your backflow device between annual tests.

First, Locate The Actual Device

Most residential backflow preventers sit near where an irrigation system connects to the main water line, often in a basement, utility area, or an above-ground enclosure near the property line. If you're not sure where yours is, that's worth figuring out before the appointment rather than having the technician spend time searching for it on arrival.

Clear Physical Access To The Device

A tester needs unobstructed access to the assembly to attach testing equipment and operate the shutoff valves on either side of it. If your device sits behind stored items, overgrown landscaping, or inside an enclosure that's been sealed shut or painted over, clearing that access before the appointment saves real time and avoids a rescheduled visit.

Make Sure The Irrigation System Is Off

Testing requires isolating the backflow assembly from both the water supply and the irrigation system temporarily. If your irrigation system is actively running or scheduled to run during the test window, that can interfere with getting an accurate reading. Confirming your irrigation controller isn't set to run during the appointment window is a simple check worth doing the night before.

Check For Any Visible Damage Or Leaks First

Before the tester arrives, take a look at the assembly for any obvious signs of damage, corrosion, or an existing leak around the fittings. If you notice something, mention it when you schedule the appointment rather than waiting for the tester to discover it, since a visibly damaged device may need parts on hand that aren't part of a routine test visit.

What Happens If The Test Fails

A failed test means one or more of the device's internal check valves isn't sealing correctly, which is a mechanical issue that develops gradually over years of normal wear rather than a sign anything was done wrong. Repair or replacement of the specific failed component resolves this in the large majority of cases, and a retest afterward confirms the fix before results are filed with the water utility.

Why This Test Exists At All

Backflow is water flowing the wrong direction through the system, potentially pulling contaminated water, like irrigation chemicals or standing water, back into the clean drinking water supply during a sudden pressure drop. The prevention assembly is what stops that from happening, and the annual test is what confirms it's still working, since a failing device gives no visible sign of the problem on its own between tests.

Does Every Property Need This?

Not every property has a backflow prevention assembly at all. It's most common on properties with an irrigation system, though certain other connections, like a boiler system or a well, can also require one depending on the setup. If you're not sure whether your property has one or needs one, that's worth confirming before assuming a testing notice doesn't apply to you.

What If You're New To The Property

If you recently purchased a home with an existing irrigation system, it's worth confirming when the backflow device was last tested, since a lapse in testing before you owned the property can still affect your standing with the water utility going forward. Requesting the prior owner's test records during a home purchase, or having a fresh test done shortly after moving in, avoids inheriting an unknown compliance gap.

A Quick Reference For Test Day

Locate the device. Clear physical access around it. Confirm the irrigation controller won't run during the test window. Look for any obvious damage beforehand and mention it when scheduling. These four items take a few minutes total and are genuinely the difference between a straightforward pass and a delayed or failed test.

A little preparation goes a long way here, and it's a small task compared to the annual peace of mind of knowing your device is confirmed working and your compliance is current with the utility.

If it's been more than a year since your last test, or you've never had one done, reaching out proactively rather than waiting for a notice from the utility keeps you ahead of any lapse.

We're happy to walk you through what to expect on the day of your specific appointment if you have any other questions beforehand.

Feel free to call us anytime with any questions about your specific backflow device or property setup, even outside of the annual testing window itself.

Keeping Up With The Schedule Going Forward

Since this is an annual requirement, setting a calendar reminder around the same time each year, rather than waiting for a notice from the utility, keeps you ahead of the deadline rather than reacting to it. Some homeowners schedule it alongside another seasonal task, like spring irrigation startup, so it's not an easy thing to forget from one year to the next.

What The Tester Actually Does During The Visit

A certified tester attaches gauges to the assembly's test ports and checks whether each internal check valve holds pressure correctly under a controlled test sequence, typically taking 15 to 30 minutes for a standard residential device. The results, pass or fail with the specific readings, are documented on a form that gets filed with your water utility, which is the official record proving your device is functioning as required for that testing cycle.

Common Reasons A Test Gets Delayed On-Site

Beyond the access and irrigation-scheduling issues covered above, a locked gate or enclosure without a key available, a pet that needs to be secured, or nobody home to let the tester into a fenced yard are all common, avoidable reasons a scheduled test doesn't happen on the first visit. Confirming these details when you schedule, not just on the day of, prevents a wasted trip for everyone involved.

What If You Just Moved Into A New Irrigation System

A newly installed irrigation system needs its backflow preventer tested for the first time shortly after installation, not just on the following year's annual cycle, since the initial test establishes the baseline the assembly is expected to maintain going forward. If you've had irrigation installed recently and aren't sure whether that initial test has happened, it's worth confirming directly rather than assuming it's already been handled as part of the installation.

Bottom Line

Locating the device, clearing access, confirming the irrigation system is off, and checking for visible damage beforehand are the four things that actually prevent a wasted appointment. None of them take more than a few minutes, and doing them ahead of time is the difference between a quick pass and a callback visit.
