What To Do When A Pipe Bursts: A Step-By-Step Guide

The first few minutes after a pipe bursts matter more than almost anything else in how much damage the event ends up causing. Here's the actual order of steps.

By the Loveland Pro Plumbing team

A burst pipe is one of those situations where most people know something needs to happen fast, but aren't sure exactly what to do first. The good news is the steps are simple and don't require any plumbing knowledge, just doing them in the right order and not wasting time.

Step 1: Shut Off The Main Water Valve

This is the single most important thing you can do, and it should happen before anything else, including calling anyone. The main shutoff valve is typically located near your water meter, in a basement, crawlspace, or utility area facing the street. Turning it off stops water flow at the source, rather than just at the fixture nearest the break, which is what actually limits how much water ends up on your floors and walls.

If you don't already know where your main shutoff is, that's worth finding out before you ever need it in an emergency. Every second spent searching for it during an actual burst pipe is water still flowing.

Step 2: Address Electrical Safety If Water Is Anywhere Near It

If water is pooling near outlets, switches, or an electrical panel, don't touch anything electrical in that area. If you can safely reach your home's main electrical panel without standing in water, shutting off power to the affected area is a reasonable precaution. If reaching it means walking through standing water, don't risk it, and mention the situation clearly when you call for help.

Step 3: Call For Emergency Plumbing Help

Once the water is shut off, call a 24/7 emergency plumber. A burst pipe won't fix itself, and depending on where the break is and how long it ran before you caught it, waiting until normal business hours can let a small problem turn into a bigger one, especially if the shutoff valve itself is old and you're not fully confident it's holding.

Step 4: Start Limiting Water Damage While You Wait

With the water off and help on the way, use the time before a technician arrives to reduce damage. Move furniture, rugs, and electronics away from wet areas if it's safe to do so. If you have a wet vac or towels available, removing standing water helps, though don't spend so much time on this that you delay calling for help in the first place.

What Happens When The Technician Arrives

A technician will confirm the shutoff is fully holding, locate the exact break, and assess whether it's a single clean section that can be cut out and replaced, or whether the surrounding pipe shows signs of broader age or damage that call for a different approach, like rerouting through a warmer, better-protected path. Either way, you'll get a flat price before any repair work starts.

Why Winter Is When This Happens Most In Loveland

Along the Front Range, the leading cause of burst pipes by far is freeze-thaw cycling. Water expands as it freezes inside a pipe, and that expansion is what splits the pipe wall, usually revealing itself only once things warm up and water starts flowing again. Exposed or poorly insulated pipe runs in crawlspaces, exterior walls, and unheated garages are the most common failure points, and this holds true whether you're in Loveland itself or nearby towns like Windsor that see the same winter conditions.

After The Repair: Don't Forget Water Damage

Fixing the pipe stops the flow, but it doesn't undo any water that already soaked into flooring, drywall, or insulation. If the burst ran for any length of time before you caught it, water damage restoration is a separate service worth having assessed, since materials that stay wet too long are a real mold risk within just a day or two.

What Not To Do

Don't wait to see if the flow stops on its own, and don't spend time trying to patch the pipe yourself before the water is shut off. A tape or clamp patch on an actively flowing burst pipe rarely holds under pressure and wastes time better spent finding and closing the main shutoff. Also avoid using any electrical appliances or tools in standing water, even briefly, no matter how urgent the situation feels.

Common Questions About Burst Pipes

How much water actually comes out of a burst pipe? More than most people expect. A typical residential supply line under normal pressure can release several gallons per minute, which adds up fast even in the few minutes it takes to locate and close a shutoff valve.

Can a burst pipe happen in warm weather too? Yes, though it's far less common. Sudden pressure spikes, physical damage during other work in the home, or a fitting that finally fails after years of slow wear can all cause a burst outside of freezing conditions.

Should I turn off my water heater too? If the burst is on a hot water line specifically, yes, shutting off the water heater's power or gas supply prevents it from continuing to heat water it can't fully deliver, which is a reasonable precaution alongside the main shutoff.

Is it normal for pipes to make noise after a repair? A brief knocking or gurgling sound as air works its way out of the lines after water service is restored is normal and usually resolves within a few minutes of running the affected fixtures.

Preventing The Next One

Once a burst is repaired, a few practical steps reduce the odds of a repeat, especially heading into another Front Range winter. Disconnecting and draining outdoor hoses before the first hard freeze, keeping cabinet doors open under sinks on exterior walls during extreme cold so warm household air can reach the pipes, and knowing your specific home's historically vulnerable spots, often called out during the repair itself, all genuinely reduce risk. None of these guarantee it won't happen again, but they meaningfully shift the odds in your favor.

What If You're Traveling When A Freeze Hits

A home left unattended during a cold snap is at higher risk simply because no one is there to catch a problem early. If you're planning to be away during winter, keeping the thermostat set no lower than the mid-50s rather than turning heat off entirely, and having a neighbor or house sitter check in periodically, are both reasonable precautions. Some homeowners also have a water sensor system installed that can alert them remotely if it detects flowing or standing water while they're away, which catches a problem hours or days sooner than discovering it after returning home.

A Word On Older Homes Specifically

Homes with original plumbing from before modern insulation codes carry more of this risk than newer construction, since pipe runs in those homes were often placed with less regard for freeze exposure than current building standards require. If your home falls into that category, an insulation and pipe-routing review during a non-emergency visit can identify vulnerable spots before winter rather than discovering them the hard way during a hard freeze.
