Bathroom Remodel? Know This About Plumbing First

The plumbing decisions in a bathroom remodel are the ones with the least flexibility to change your mind later. Here's what to lock in early.

By the Loveland Pro Plumbing team

Most bathroom remodels start with the fun parts: tile samples, fixture finishes, and layout inspiration. The plumbing underneath all of that deserves attention earlier in the process than most homeowners give it, since it's far more expensive to change after tile and drywall are already in than to plan correctly from the start.

Decide Early: Are You Moving Anything?

Keeping the toilet, sink, and tub or shower in their existing locations keeps the plumbing rough-in essentially unchanged, which is the least expensive path. Moving any of these, especially the toilet, means relocating drain lines, which is a meaningfully bigger job than most homeowners initially expect, since it often means breaking into the slab or subfloor rather than a simple reconnect. If budget is a real constraint, keeping fixture locations unchanged is the single biggest cost-saving decision in the whole project.

Tub-To-Shower Conversions Need Planning Too

Converting a bathtub to a walk-in shower, a common remodel goal, still involves real plumbing work even though it looks like a surface-level change. The drain typically needs to move and be resized for the new shower base, and the water supply valve often needs to be relocated and raised to a height appropriate for standing rather than sitting. Planning the shower installation alongside the rest of the remodel, rather than as an afterthought, avoids surprises once demolition starts.

Water Heater Capacity Is Worth Reconsidering

If your remodel includes a larger shower, a rain shower head, or multiple shower heads, it's worth checking whether your existing water heater can actually keep up with the new fixture's demand. A remodel that upgrades the shower experience but leaves an undersized water heater in place often ends with disappointing hot water performance despite an otherwise successful renovation. This is a good time to evaluate whether a larger tank or a tankless upgrade makes sense alongside the rest of the work.

New Fixtures Mean New Supply Line Connections

Every new faucet, toilet, and shower valve needs its own supply line connection, and reusing old, corroded shutoff valves rather than replacing them during a remodel is a common source of leaks discovered after the project is otherwise finished. Since walls and floors are already open during a remodel, replacing old valves and supply lines while everything is accessible is far cheaper than doing it as a separate repair after the remodel is sealed back up.

Permits Aren't Optional For Real Plumbing Changes

Moving drain lines, relocating a toilet, or adding a new fixture typically requires a permit and inspection, even for a remodel that otherwise feels like a cosmetic update. Skipping this step to save time is the kind of shortcut that surfaces as a real problem during a future home sale, when a buyer's inspector or title company asks for permit records that don't exist for work that clearly happened. Any relocated drain or supply line still has to meet the same Uniform Plumbing Code (UPC) requirements for slope, venting, and fixture spacing that the city inspects new construction against.

Coordinating Plumbing With Other Trades

A bathroom remodel usually involves plumbing, electrical, and tile or flooring work in some sequence, and plumbing rough-in generally needs to happen before flooring and tile go in, not after. If you're managing the project yourself rather than through a single general contractor, confirming this sequencing with each trade ahead of time avoids the expensive mistake of finishing a floor before the plumber has finished work that requires access underneath it.

What About A Bathroom In An Older Home

If you're remodeling a bathroom in an older home, especially one in an established neighborhood like Berthoud's historic downtown, it's worth having the existing supply and drain lines assessed before finalizing the remodel plan. Discovering aging galvanized pipe or a deteriorating cast iron drain mid-remodel, after walls are already open, is a far more disruptive time to find out than knowing upfront and planning for a partial repipe as part of the project from the beginning.

A Practical Planning Order

Decide on fixture locations first. Confirm water heater capacity for the planned fixtures. Have existing supply and drain lines assessed if the home is older. Get permits squared away before demolition starts. Then move on to the tile and finish selections that are genuinely more fun to think about, now with a plumbing plan that won't get disrupted partway through.

Budgeting For The Plumbing Portion Realistically

Plumbing typically represents a meaningful share of a full bathroom remodel's total budget, more if fixtures are being relocated or the home needs supply line updates, less if everything stays in place and only fixtures themselves are being swapped. Getting a plumbing-specific quote early in the planning process, before finalizing the overall remodel budget, prevents the plumbing line item from becoming a surprise that forces cuts elsewhere in the project later.

Ventilation Matters More Than It Seems

A bathroom exhaust fan is often overlooked during remodel planning since it's not directly plumbing, but it's closely related to moisture management in a room that's about to get new drywall, paint, and possibly new flooring. An underpowered or poorly vented fan lets moisture linger longer than it should, which can affect everything from paint longevity to the long-term condition of any wood-adjacent materials in the room. If your remodel is touching the ceiling anyway, upgrading the exhaust fan's capacity is a low-cost addition worth considering.

What If You're Adding A Second Bathroom Fixture Set

Adding a second sink, or converting a half bath into a full bath with a new shower, is a bigger plumbing project than remodeling an existing full bathroom, since it usually requires extending both supply and drain lines to a location that never had them before. This is worth scoping with a plumber very early, even before finalizing the room layout, since the practical path for running new lines sometimes constrains which wall the new fixtures can realistically go against.

Final Walkthrough Before Sign-Off

Once the plumbing work is complete, running every fixture at full pressure simultaneously and checking every new connection for even a slow drip is worth doing before tile grout and paint make future access harder. Catching a slow leak at a fitting during this walkthrough, rather than weeks later, saves you from discovering it as water damage in a freshly finished room.

A remodel you're excited about deserves plumbing that holds up quietly in the background for years, not a surprise repair call within the first year of enjoying the new space.

Taking the time to get these details right before demolition starts is what separates a remodel that goes smoothly from one that runs into expensive surprises halfway through.

We're glad to walk through your specific layout and fixture plans before you finalize anything, so the plumbing side is settled well ahead of demo day.

Bottom Line

The plumbing decisions in a bathroom remodel are the ones with the least room to change your mind after the fact. Locking those in early, even before you've picked a tile pattern, is what keeps the rest of the project on budget and on schedule.
