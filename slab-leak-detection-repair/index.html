Signs Of A Slab Leak

What Makes This Different From A Normal Leak

Warm Spot On The Floor

Heat from a leaking hot water line transferring through the slab.

Unexplained High Water Bill

A jump in usage with a meter that keeps moving even with everything off.

Cracking In Flooring Or Walls

Water affecting soil stability under the foundation over time.

Sound Of Running Water Underfoot

An audible trickle with no visible source anywhere above the slab.

Why This Needs Different Equipment

Concrete Changes How We Detect It

General leak detection relies heavily on acoustic and thermal methods that work well through drywall and framing, but concrete transmits sound and heat differently than those materials do. A slab leak needs equipment specifically tuned for how a leak sounds and reads through a concrete foundation, which is why this gets treated as its own diagnostic category rather than folded into standard leak detection. Getting the location wrong on a slab means opening concrete in the wrong spot, which is a mistake worth avoiding with the right equipment upfront.

Repair Options

Reroute Vs. Slab Access

Once a slab leak is located, rerouting the damaged section above the slab, running new pipe through a wall or ceiling instead of under the foundation, avoids breaking concrete in many cases and is often the preferred fix. A tunnel underneath the slab or direct slab access is sometimes still necessary depending on where the leak sits and how the home's plumbing is laid out, and we'll walk through which option applies before recommending either one.

Slabs And Shifting Soil

Why Expansive Clay Matters Here

Slab-on-grade foundations sit directly on soil, and the expansive clay common along the Front Range swells when wet and shrinks when dry. That movement can stress lines under a slab over the years. Homes in newer areas like Centerra and east Loveland are more likely to be slab-on-grade, while many older homes near downtown have basements or crawl spaces instead.

Access And Aftermath

Driveways, Garage Slabs, And Water Coming Up Through The Floor

Not every slab leak sits under the living room. Garage floors, patios, and driveway slabs carry supply and drain lines too, and a leak at the slab edge can push water toward the foundation wall. Water seeping up through concrete or pooling along a baseboard deserves a prompt look, even when the amount seems small.

Can The Concrete Be Opened?

Sometimes yes, and sometimes a reroute through the attic or walls is the smarter plan. Opening a slab means cutting a small window over the confirmed spot and patching the concrete after the repair. A reroute leaves the floor alone but takes more labor above it. Both options are priced before work starts, and the right one depends on where the line runs and what covers the floor.

When A Reroute Becomes A Repipe

A house that has leaked under the slab more than once may do better with a full reroute of its supply system than with repeated spot repairs. That conversation belongs with the repiping page and begins only after the first leak is located.

How Close Is The Pinpoint?

Callers often ask whether imaging gear is part of the visit. It is, alongside acoustic listening, and a tight pinpoint keeps the opening small, which keeps cost and mess down. The general search methods are described on the leak detection page.
