What's Actually Wrong

Flapper, Fill Valve, Or Wax Ring

A toilet that runs after every flush is almost always the flapper or the fill valve, both inexpensive parts that wear out over years of normal use and are usually swapped in a single visit. A toilet that rocks when you sit on it is a different problem entirely. That usually means the wax ring under the base has been compromised, often from the toilet having been loose for a while before anyone noticed, which lets water seep out at the base and can damage the subfloor if it goes unaddressed.

Older toilets installed before the mid-1990s often use well over three gallons per flush, compared to the 1.28 to 1.6 gallons a modern low-flow model uses. That difference adds up over a year, and it's part of why a full replacement sometimes pencils out even on a toilet that isn't actually broken, especially paired with a repair that was already needed anyway. A weak flush on an older toilet is sometimes a clogged jet or a worn flush valve rather than the low-flow design itself, so it's worth having that checked before assuming the toilet simply needs to be replaced.

Repair Vs. Replace

When A New Toilet Makes More Sense

Most running-toilet, weak-flush, and wax-ring problems are worth repairing rather than replacing, especially on a toilet under 10-15 years old. A cracked tank or bowl, a toilet that's failed more than once in the past year, or an older high-water-usage model are the situations where a full replacement is the better value, and newer models use meaningfully less water per flush.

Old Toilets, New Toilets

What The 1994 Water Standard Changed

Federal rules have limited new toilets to 1.6 gallons per flush since 1994. Homes in Historic Downtown Loveland and West Central Loveland that still have their original toilets may use far more water per flush, while newer homes in Centerra already have low-flow models. Either way, a running toilet or a weak flush is usually a small, inexpensive repair.

Parts And Models

Flush Levers, Wax Rings, And Smart Toilet Seats

Plenty of toilet calls trace to one small part. A snapped flush lever on a one-piece tank can make a working toilet look dead. A failed wax ring shows up as a damp floor around the base. Describing the symptom precisely often saves a second trip.

Lever Snapped

The tank has water but the handle no longer moves the flapper.

Water At The Base

Dampness around the floor line usually means a failed seal.

Runs At Random

A leaking flapper or fill valve can quietly add to the water bill.

Backs Up Or Gurgles

That is a drain issue, so see the drain cleaning page.

Smart Seats And Bidet Wands

Electric seats and wash wands add wiring and a water inlet to the usual tank and bowl. Share the brand and model before the visit. Whether a given brand can be repaired on site is confirmed on the phone, and manufacturer parts may need to be ordered.

Installing A New Toilet

A replacement quote should say whether it includes removing the old toilet, a new wax ring, and a fresh supply line. Three elongated bowls are a different job from one standard bowl. Give the count and the rough-in distance if you know it.

Running Toilets And A Rising Bill

Two toilets that run at random can add a surprising amount to a monthly bill. If the bill is still climbing after the flappers are fixed, the leak detection page explains what to check next.
